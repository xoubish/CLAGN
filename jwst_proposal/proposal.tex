\scientificjustification

\noindent\textbf{Does the dust around a changing nucleus keep up with it?}
An active galactic nucleus (AGN) can undergo large luminosity changes within years [1,2], sometimes accompanied by a transformation of its continuum and broad-line spectrum [3--5]. Its surrounding dust records these changes through delayed heating and through destruction or replenishment of the emitting material. Individual nuclei show that dust boundaries evolve over years [6,7]; the next step is to establish how the response depends on the direction and history of nuclear activity. Changing-look and strongly variable AGN followed through rises, declines, flares and recoveries provide this experiment.

We request \textbf{67.0~h of MIRI/MRS spectroscopy of 24 AGN at $z<0.3$} with recorded rises, declines, flares and recoveries. ZTF/WISE monitoring, SPHEREx coverage and optical spectroscopy, including 2026 Palomar/NGPS observations, connect nuclear activity to the hot-dust response. Together, these histories sample different stages of change. MIRI supplies the essential warm continuum, both silicate features and spatial constraints on host emission, connecting each history to its warm-dust spectrum. The programme addresses two linked questions:

\noindent\textbf{1. How strongly does the warm spectrum remember the nuclear history?} Delayed heating can produce a warm excess after a decline and a deficit after a rise [8,9]. The recorded histories, anchored by the 2010 WISE measurements, test whether this spectral memory persists at the MIRI epoch.

\noindent\textbf{2. Does the dust boundary move, or only its temperature?} Delayed heating of unchanged dust is the null model. The silicate features and the full 5--28~$\mu$m continuum test whether the spectra require changes in the dust distribution beyond this response [6--9].

\noindent\textbf{From infrared echoes to dust evolution.}
WISE observations have established infrared echoes of changing-look transitions [10--13], supporting intrinsic changes in nuclear power in those sources. Broadband colours, however, combine temperature, emitting area, extinction and host light [14--16]. The proposed spectra test the warm spectral signature of those histories and constrain which properties of the emitting material produce it.

The equilibrium sublimation radius scales approximately as $L^{1/2}$ [17--19], but the physical dust boundary can adjust over years [6]. Individual nuclei already suggest different responses: the smaller dust radius after Mrk~590 faded supports replenishment [7], whereas the temperature variations in Mrk~817 favour heating and cooling at a nearly fixed boundary [20]. These results motivate a comparison across both directions of change. Recovering sources further test whether the spectrum depends on the path to its present state.

\noindent\textbf{MIRI's diagnostic power.}
SPHEREx covers 0.75--5~$\mu$m [21], constraining hot dust close to the nucleus [20,22]. Longer delays and weaker variability at longer wavelengths [8,23,24] make the warm continuum sensitive to earlier illumination. MRS supplies the essential 4.9--27.9~$\mu$m spectrum [25]: the continuum measures the imprint, the paired silicate features constrain geometry and optical depth [26,27], and spatial profiles and aromatic bands constrain host emission [16,28,29]. MIRI extends the NEOWISE/SPHEREx record into the warm-dust regime and connects it to the 2010 W3/W4 baseline. This combination tests how dust at different temperatures responds to the same recorded activity.

\noindent\textbf{A sample selected to test dust memory.}
Our targets span sustained rises, declines, flares, plateaus and recoveries, testing the direction, amplitude and timing of the dust response. Recovering sources ask whether nuclei at comparable current power retain different warm spectra because they followed different paths. We combine catalogued changing-look AGN [30--33] with large-amplitude ZTF/WISE variables [5,34], selecting across spectral classifications [31,35]. A variability manifold identifies candidates with similar light-curve shapes [36]; available multi-epoch spectra establish continuum and broad-line changes. Visual inspection reveals no obvious merger signatures or very close projected companions. Each target has approximately a decade of W1/W2 monitoring, with 95th-to-5th-percentile flux ratios of 1.3--2.3 before host subtraction. Usable SPHEREx measurements supplement 23 histories; the remaining crowded source retains the WISE constraint on near-infrared variability.

\noindent\textbf{From dust components to their response.}
Published observations demonstrate the three elements of our experiment: separating hot and warm dust (Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}A), constraining central and surrounding emission (B), and interpreting a delayed infrared response using the activity history (C). Each MRS cube supplies the warm continuum, both silicate profiles, and PAH and spatial diagnostics of host emission. The rest-frame 8--13~$\mu$m dust luminosity includes broad silicate emission or absorption, with PAHs and narrow lines fitted separately. Continuum slopes and paired silicate profiles constrain the temperature distribution, optical depth and geometry, including when an integrated flux is consistent with its historical value.

\noindent\textbf{Connecting spectral shape to the recorded history.}
We will jointly fit the warm spectrum, current nuclear power and each source's full light curve, including reversals. Optical decomposition, WISE and SPHEREx constrain illumination and hot dust at their observed epochs [4,20,22]; optical monitoring near JWST anchors the current state. Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}C illustrates why earlier illumination matters: the warm response can be delayed and broadened. Our single MRS visit tests whether spectral residuals at comparable current power depend on the recorded history, weighted by the model's distribution of light-travel delays; it does not directly measure a lag. Sustained changes, flares and recoveries enter through their measured histories. The 2010 W3/W4 measurements provide additional constraints through their bandpasses and a shared host model. Event dates, gaps and the optical-to-heating conversion enter as uncertainties; an old flare need not leave a detectable warm imprint.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement precision and interpretation.}
A separate illustrative shell-model calculation for the rising source in Figure~\ref{fig:history} (bottom), using dust opacities from [37], gives approximately 5\% precision on the extracted 8--13~$\mu$m flux and uncertainties of 0.08 and 0.04 on local logarithmic silicate contrasts at 9.7 and 18~$\mu$m. These estimates assume the requested S/N, correlated calibration errors of 3\% in scale and tilt, and a smooth host residual of 10\% at rest 12~$\mu$m. Both fixed and evolving dust fit the simulated spectrum. These conditional precision estimates do not establish model discrimination; Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics} instead demonstrates the observed diagnostics that support our joint analysis.

\noindent\textbf{Testing whether delayed heating is sufficient.}
Clumpy and disc-plus-wind geometries [38--40] supply responses to the reconstructed histories [8,9], with composition and grain-size uncertainty [41]. Fixed distributions change temperature; evolving distributions also change the amount or location of dust [6,7,22]. Both include light-travel delays. We summarize the warm residual by $\delta_{\rm warm}=\log_{10}[L^{\rm MRS}_{8\text{--}13\,\mu{\rm m}}/L^{\rm fixed}_{8\text{--}13\,\mu{\rm m}}(t_{\rm MIRI})]$, relative to the \emph{delayed fixed-distribution} prediction. Evidence for evolution requires joint continuum and silicate discrepancies across the allowed fixed models [28], robust to host and calibration uncertainties [42], and an improved fit from an evolving distribution. Where both families fit, we will report bounds on the warm response and permitted evolution. These constraints also test infrared estimates of nuclear power and covering factor [43,44].

\noindent\textbf{Complementary tests of obscuration and excitation.}
Paired silicate profiles, optical histories and high-ionization lines constrain obscuration and excitation [26,27]. [Ne\,\textsc{vi}] 7.7 and [Ne\,\textsc{v}] 14.3~$\mu$m are accessible throughout the sample [45]; the nominal MRS range includes [Ne\,\textsc{v}] 24.3~$\mu$m for 13 targets and [O\,\textsc{iv}] 25.9~$\mu$m for nine. Line detections or limits provide supporting diagnostics; they are not instantaneous measures of nuclear power.

\noindent\textbf{Central and surrounding dust.}
Where extended emission is detected, we will compare central and surrounding spectra at a common spatial resolution within the overlapping field, reporting the physical scales sampled. PAH 11.3/7.7~$\mu$m ratios, continuum slopes and silicate strengths probe dust processing, heating and obscuration [29,46]. The decomposition in Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}B demonstrates how surrounding emission can be isolated to constrain host templates [28], testing whether a central warm excess survives allowed host contributions. This measures the resolved dust environment; the variable dust remains unresolved.

\descriptionofobservations

\noindent\textbf{Spectral coverage and sensitivity.}
We request one MIRI/MRS science visit and a matching sky visit per target. All three grating settings, nuclear target acquisition and a four-point dither provide approximately 4.9--27.9~$\mu$m coverage [25,47]. Both silicate peaks fall within this range for all 24 targets. The primary rest-frame 8--13~$\mu$m continuum diagnostic is accessible throughout the sample without channel~4C; the full spectrum constrains the longer-wavelength continuum and 18~$\mu$m profile.

Archival total-source W3 and W4 fluxes span 1.9--48.4 and 6.4--82.6~mJy. We target continuum S/N of 50 per $R=100$ bin near rest 5 and 12~$\mu$m and 30 around the 18~$\mu$m feature, corresponding to 2\% and 3.3\% statistical errors per bin. We use FULL/FASTR1, one integration per dither, and 27/60/27 groups in settings A/B/C for 23 targets. Four dithers give 299.7/666.0/299.7~s on source, with all four channels observed simultaneously. The W3-faint J083826.50+371906.7 uses 80 groups in B (888.0~s). Equal-depth sky exposures preserve extended host emission for decomposition [47]. Science-plus-sky integration totals 17.00~h. \textbf{We request 67.0~h}, including acquisition, visit operations, slews and observatory overheads calculated with APT~2026.5.1: a Medium programme [48].

For J083826.50+371906.7 at $z=0.2111$, ETC/Pandeia~2026.7 predicts S/N of 55/225/61 per $R=100$ bin at rest 5/12/18~$\mu$m. These estimates assume a point source with $F_\nu\propto\lambda^2$, 0.95~mJy in W3 (half the archival total), the adopted sky spectrum, equal-depth sky exposures and extraction radii of $0.3''$/$0.6''$/$0.9''$. They establish statistical sensitivity under the adopted nuclear-flux model; host and calibration uncertainties enter the science inference separately. Acquisition uses F560W for the faintest sources and the neutral-density filter for brighter nuclei, with readouts giving S/N above 50 without saturation over the adopted flux ranges [49].

\noindent\textbf{Connecting the observing epochs.}
The historical baseline gives each MIRI spectrum its temporal context. WISE and SPHEREx constrain earlier dust states; 2010 AllWISE W3/W4 photometry supplies a warm-emission anchor. Optical monitoring near JWST constrains the contemporary nucleus. One MIRI visit per target provides a warm spectral snapshot across 24 histories, with sensitivity to their direction, amplitude and event age. Forward modelling preserves each target's distinct illumination history and geometry.

\noindent\textbf{Separating nuclear dust from the host.}
\textbf{Each MRS cube measures the nucleus and surrounding host together.} The PSF FWHM grows from approximately $0.3''$ at 5~$\mu$m to $1''$ at 25~$\mu$m, sampling roughly 0.1--4.5~kpc across the sample; channel fields span $3.2''\!\times3.7''$ to $6.6''\!\times7.7''$ [25]. Wavelength-dependent PSF fitting [50] separates unresolved and resolved emission [28]. Surrounding spectra constrain host templates with allowance for spatial variation. Joint modelling separates continuum, silicate, PAH and H$_2$ contributions [15,46]; PAHs allow for AGN processing [29], while [Ne\,\textsc{v}] and [Ne\,\textsc{vi}] trace highly ionised gas [45]. Alternative extractions and host templates [16] bound the nuclear flux, including compact host light within the unresolved component.

We will compare the 2010 W3/W4 photometry with redshifted nuclear-plus-host models integrated through the WISE bandpasses, including colour corrections and emission outside MRS coverage. Archival imaging constrains host light beyond the MRS field and within the SPHEREx aperture. Both science tests propagate nuclear-flux bounds and wavelength-dependent calibration covariance [42].

\supplementalinformation
\noindent\textbf{Special requirements.} Each science/sky pair is linked as a consecutive, noninterruptible sequence to obtain a contemporaneous background [47]. Sky fields are selected using WISE and 2MASS catalogues and images, with at least $25''$ clearance from catalogued sources. Twenty-three fields lie within 1--2~arcmin of their nuclei; the crowded field around J20145928+2523010 uses a $135''$ offset. Background targets are marked extended and use four-point extended-source dithers, with no target acquisition. No fixed-date constraints, coordinated parallels or joint-facility time are requested. APT's Visit Planner returns scheduling windows for all 48 visits.

\noindent\textbf{Duplications.} A coordinate-based MAST search on 2026 September 26, including archived and planned JWST observations, found no overlapping observations within $120''$ of any of the 24 science positions. No potential MIRI/MRS duplications were identified.
